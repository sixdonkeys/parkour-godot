# Goal

You are the sole engineer on this build, for a father and son who want a parkour game they can race each other in on the son's MacBook Neo. Build an original 3D parkour game in Godot 4 from this empty folder: timed runs through four invented levels (a desert level that looks like the country around Tucson, Arizona, from the desert floor through a canyon and up into its mountains; a mountain that looks like the Black Hills of South Dakota; rooftops in Chicago; rooftops in New York, both cities as they are today), each designed as a parkour course first and then dressed to look like that real place, playable in first and third person with a switch mid-run, with a character whose look the player can change, checkpoints, instant restart, medals, saved best times, ghost racing, free roam and a photo mode. The target machine is a MacBook Neo (macOS, Apple A18 Pro, 8 GB of memory shared with graphics, 2408×1506 display); development happens on Apple-silicon machines running Fedora Asahi Linux and macOS. The game must also run well on a ThinkPad T14 (CachyOS on x86_64, AMD graphics). There is no comparator game, and this is not a recreation of one. The bar for looks is photography: `reference/` holds photographs of those four places, and the critic grades against those files, not against a memory of any game. The bar for play is measured: every route is proved completable by a recorded run, and difficulty, flow and responsiveness are numbers in a log. The quality floor: any frame of a run could be paused and pass for a photograph taken somewhere real, and every move responds at once and fails only when the player erred. The module list in item 1 is the scope; nothing outside it, so no level editor, story or purchases. Multiplayer, several people in the same world over a local network or Tailscale, is not built in this pass, but it is coming, and nothing in the design may rule it out. The work is ordered so that research comes before choosing, a critic that didn't build the thing stands between the builder and the word done, and evidence is on disk before any claim.

The game must look and feel like a AAA title, within the boundaries this brief sets on performance and hardware (Nick, 2026-11-15). That raises no budget number and loosens none: where a choice trades effort for polish (animation, camera feel, sound, materials, lighting, interface), the default is the AAA option that fits the budget, not the first one that works, and the critic scores against that bar.

## Amendment 1 — a lighter process (Nick, 2026-11-15)

Nick's words: "Fix all this shit about our process." Where this amendment and the text below disagree, this amendment wins. It applies to the builder and the critic alike. Everything not named here stands, including the budgets, the course and window rules, the asset policy, the Neo testing and the playtests.

1. **Desert first, by milestones.** The work runs to milestones, not module by module:
   - **M1, first playable:** movement, camera, traversal, plus an early movement-only playtest build as soon as a camera follows the body.
   - **M2, the desert course playable start to finish:** kit, rules, interface, desert-course.
   - **M3, the desert complete:** character, desert-dress.
   - **M4–M6:** mountain, Chicago and New York, each course then dress.
   - **M7:** final.

   The other three worlds start only after M3 ("we can build the other 3 worlds after the first is done"). The build stops at the end of each milestone for Nick's playtest, and Nick starts the next milestone. Item 7's stages map to these milestones.
2. **Parallel builders.** The main builder may hand independent modules to Fable agents working in their own git worktrees. They share a GPU lock (`.local/gpu.lock`) for anything that draws or measures, and a Neo lock (`.local/neo.lock`). The main builder merges their branches, keeps the docs current, and warns Nick before running more than two agents at once. Item 3's "one agent builds" no longer holds.
3. **Reviews at milestones, not per module.** A fresh critic grades once per milestone, covering every module in it, with up to 3 rounds per milestone (not 4 per module). Look and feel modules (movement, camera, traversal, character, every -course and -dress) are scored as item 4 says and close at 8.5. Tool modules (verify, core, kit instruments, rules plumbing, interface plumbing) are pass/fail: they work, their instruments are trusted, their budgets hold. `look-slice` gets its single round inside M1. Between milestones, the automatic checks run before each merge: the presets that touch what changed, plus the frame, memory and fuzz budgets.
4. **Lighter decision records.** A record needs Options, Choice, Reason and Licence, plus its Sources as links. Quote word for word only the line or lines the choice rests on, inline in the record. Separate `docs/survey/` files are optional. Records already written stand as they are.
5. **Prove an instrument once.** Each instrument is proved on its known-answer fixture when it is built and whenever it changes. At a milestone the critic re-proves only instruments that changed since the last milestone, plus one spot check of its own choosing.
6. **Window tests twice.** The full window sequence under GNOME, niri and Umbriel runs at M1 and at the final gate. The GNOME window check stays in the automatic checks.
7. **Leaner evidence in git.** Commit each run's `log.json` summary and the stills a critic grades. Raw engine output, frame strips and WAVs stay on disk (git-ignored), named by preset and commit so they can be made again. Evidence folders are per milestone (`docs/evidence/<module>/<milestone>/`).
8. **Neo runs** at each milestone, and whenever a change aimed at performance lands, not for every commit.

This brief is the contract, and it lives at `BUILD-BRIEF.md` in the project root. If you were handed it as text, save it there word for word before anything else. The builder never edits it. If the folder holds anything else when you start (hidden tool folders such as `.git` and `.claude` aside) and there is no `docs/STATUS.json`, stop and tell Nick: this build starts from an empty folder. If the folder isn't a git repository yet, make it one, on a branch named `main`, before writing the first file.

# How to work

1. **Architecture first.** Before any feature code, write `ARCHITECTURE.md`. The engine's project root is `game/`, with one folder per subsystem inside it; `reference/`, `docs/` and `builds/` sit outside it, so nothing in them is ever imported or shipped. The subsystems:
   - `core`: run state, the game loop, saved data, events, the window and the quality profile.
   - `movement`: the player's body and its momentum, independent of any camera.
   - `traversal`: finding and performing vaults, ledge climbs, wall runs, slides and landing rolls.
   - `camera`: the first-person and third-person views and the switch between them.
   - `character`: the human model, its animation, how hands and feet meet the world, and the looks a player can choose for it.
   - `kit`: parkour pieces sized from measured movement numbers, difficulty grades, moving hazards, and the tools for laying out a gray-block course.
   - `rules`: checkpoints, timer, restart, medals, best times, ghosts, collectibles.
   - `interface`: menus, heads-up display, settings, the screen where a player changes their character's look, photo mode, free roam, the credits screen, sound.
   - For each environment a `-course` module, the gray-block course with no scenery, and a `-dress` module, the terrain, materials, lighting, sky and set dressing laid over that same course: `desert-course`, `desert-dress`, `mountain-course`, `mountain-dress`, `chicago-course`, `chicago-dress`, `newyork-course`, `newyork-dress`. The environments are `desert` (the Sonoran Desert country around Tucson, Arizona, which gets mountainous: the level climbs from the desert floor, through a canyon section, into terrain like the road up Mount Lemmon, and includes a rock outcrop of the kind at Javelina Rocks Overlook, invented, not copied), `mountain` (the Black Hills of South Dakota), `chicago` and `newyork` (each city as it is today). Every course is invented, not a copy of real trails or streets; what is real is the look: the landforms, rock, plants, light, building types, materials and rooftop details of that place, for the two terrain levels the broad shape of the land, and for the two cities the skyline and the pattern of the blocks. Chicago and New York must be told apart at a glance by someone who knows both.

   The worlds are large. `desert` and `mountain` each have ground the player can roam about 1 km on a side; `chicago` and `newyork` each have about 400 m on a side of rooftops that can be run, some 6 to 12 blocks. Beyond that the place carries on to the horizon as backdrop the player can see and not reach: the real mountain above the desert level, the rest of the skyline around the rooftops. Each main route is 1.5 to 2.5 km long, a run of three to five minutes. Real heights are compressed: the desert level climbs some 150 to 300 m, not the 2,000 m of the real mountain. The quality floor holds along every route and at every challenge spot. Off them, free roam must still read as the place, with no holes, bare patches or visible edge of the world, though it may repeat more. These sizes are targets that `look-slice` tests on the Neo before any level is built; if the Neo can't carry them, that is a conflict for `docs/WAITING.md`.

   The worlds are generated once, when the game is built, and then fixed. Nothing about a level is generated while the game runs, and every player on every machine gets the same world. The broad shape of the two terrain levels starts from the United States Geological Survey's public-domain elevation data for the real place, the country around Tucson for `desert` and the Black Hills for `mountain`, compressed to the sizes above; this is Nick's choice and stays fixed. Rules and a seed then place what hand work can't cover at this size: the scatter of rock, cactus, grass and trees by slope and height, and the bulk of the city blocks from building parts. The two cities start from real data as well. The skyline backdrop is the real one, from the Survey's public-domain lidar scans. The rooftops that can be run are invented blocks in the city's real pattern, not a copy of a real neighbourhood, so the course is free to be designed for parkour first: their block sizes, street grid and spread of building heights come from the lidar and from the building records Chicago and New York publish, where those records' terms fit the asset policy. OpenStreetMap and other data under share-alike terms are not used. The routes, the challenge spots, the landmarks and anything a height map can't hold (cliffs, overhangs, the outcrop) are designed on top. The generator, its seed, and the address and checksum of its source data are in the repository, so the same world can be made again on any machine. The elevation data is recorded in `ASSETS.md` like any other asset, with its public-domain statement quoted from the Survey.

   `ARCHITECTURE.md` also holds a shared data model, the public API each module exposes, the events it emits, units and conventions (metres, seconds and kilograms, one engine unit to the metre; everything else on the hard-problems list below is decided there, not here), determinism (seeded RNG only; a test mode with fixed timing; a recorded run replays to the same result on the same machine), the definition of every measured event in terms of the engine state its instrument reads, for each hard problem the first module that depends on it, the course rules, the window rules, the performance budget, the dependency and asset policy, and failure isolation so a level or optional module that fails to load returns the player to the menu with the error logged and never stops the game starting.

   `ARCHITECTURE.md` is kept current, not written once. A hard problem's record is researched and written when the first module that depends on it starts, not all of them up front. Until then its entry in `ARCHITECTURE.md` reads "undecided" and names that module, and the commit that completes the record updates `ARCHITECTURE.md` to carry the choice.

   The course rules:
   - Every obstacle is sized from the movement numbers in `docs/metrics.json` (longest jump at a full sprint, highest ledge reach, longest wall run, top speed), which are fixed when `traversal` closes.
   - Grades: green tests up to 65% of the limit, blue up to 85%, black above that, or any chain of three or more moves with nowhere to stand between them.
   - Each level has a main route of green and blue obstacles only, at least two black lines (optional shortcuts that beat the main route's time), and at least three challenge spots off the main route, each holding a collectible that the level's results screen counts.
   - Anything that moves belongs in the place: a crumbling ledge, a rolling log, a swinging crane hook.

   The window rules, so the game is tied to no window manager. It must sit as well in tiling ones such as Niri and Umbriel, which give a window whatever size and shape they choose, as in GNOME; those three are the ones checked:
   - Any size or shape the window is given is taken at once: the picture fills it without stretching and the interface stays readable and inside it, from a narrow column to a wide strip. The game never asks for a size, a position or a display mode of its own.
   - One window, with a stable application ID and title that window rules can match. No splash or helper windows.
   - Fullscreen is a toggle, and leaving it returns the window to its tile.
   - The pointer is held only during play in a focused window. Pausing, opening a menu or losing focus lets it go at once, and losing focus pauses a run.
   - The window manager's shortcuts always get through: nothing is bound to the Super key and the keyboard is never grabbed.

   The performance budget, each number with the first module at which its instrument has something to measure:
   - Frame time: on the `neo` quality profile at the Neo's display resolution, 95% of frames within 16.7 ms and 99% within 22 ms on every benchmark path and recorded run (from `core` on an empty scene; binding for each level from its `-course` module).
   - Memory: process plus video memory at most 4 GB with any one level loaded, and back within 5% of the pre-load figure once the level is unloaded (from `core`).
   - Build size: the macOS build at most 4 GB (from `core`).
   - Input to motion: at most 50 ms at the 95th percentile, from the engine receiving the input to the body's first movement on screen (from `movement`).
   - Fuzz: 10 minutes of seeded random input ending with zero falls through the world, zero stuck states and zero non-finite values (from `movement`, on the metrics course).
   - Route completion: every main route's recorded run completes 20 times out of 20 with each input's timing jittered by a seeded amount of up to 16 ms either way; every black line's and challenge spot's recorded run completes unjittered (from `kit`).
   - Input rate: no recorded run changes more than 8 actions in any one second (from `kit`).
   - Leeway, the spread of take-off timing that still succeeds: at least 150 ms on every main-route obstacle, 50 ms on black lines, 33 ms on challenge spots (from `kit`).
   - Grades: every obstacle's grade, recomputed from its measured size and `docs/metrics.json`, matches the grade it carries (from `kit`).
   - Restart: a fall to back in control at the last checkpoint within 1 s (from `rules`).
   - Flow: at least 60% of each main route's recorded run at or above 70% of top speed, with no stop longer than 1 s (from `desert-course`).
   - Soak: 10 minutes looping a level ends with frame time and memory within 10% of its first minute (from `desert-course`).
   - Window: through a resize across every window-shape preset, a focus loss and a fullscreen toggle during a recorded run, zero engine errors, zero interface elements outside the window, and the pointer released and the run paused on the focus loss (from `core`; the interface check from `interface`).
   - Two players: two bodies in one level, each driven by a different recorded run and each with its own timer, checkpoint and collectibles, both finish with zero engine errors (from `rules`).

   The Neo is the machine these numbers are for. A number measured on a development machine stands in only under the `neo-proxy` decision below, is labelled proxy in the log, is never judged more leniently than the budget itself, and gives way to the Neo's own number as soon as a result folder from it exists. The ThinkPad T14 must meet the same numbers on the same profile at its own display resolution; where the two machines pull in different directions the Neo wins, and the conflict goes into `docs/WAITING.md`.

   The dependency and asset policy: game code in GDScript only; third-party code under MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD, zlib or CC0; a native plugin only if it supplies or builds for macOS arm64, Linux arm64 and Linux x86_64 and does not rule out Windows x86_64; shipped assets public domain, CC0, or under a licence that asks for nothing beyond credit (CC BY and the like), with every credit owed shown on a credits screen in the game; share-alike and non-commercial licences stay out of what ships; every dependency and asset recorded in `ASSETS.md` with its source address, author, licence and the licence statement quoted from the source. Every setting that trades looks for speed (render scale, lighting method, shadows, texture and mesh detail, effects) lives in a quality profile, one file per profile; the game ships one profile, `neo`; source art is kept at the best quality obtained and reduced per profile.

   Once `ARCHITECTURE.md` exists, the budget numbers, the course rules and the window rules are the contract. Tightening one is a decision with its own record. Loosening one is Nick's call: write the case into `docs/WAITING.md` and keep working to the current number. The leeway, flow and input-rate numbers are starting values he may move after a playtest. If a decision record conflicts with something this brief fixes, the brief wins and the conflict goes into `docs/WAITING.md`.

   Also write `docs/CRITIC-PROMPT.md`, the whole prompt the critic runs from, with exactly this text: "You are the critic for this build. You write no product code. Read `BUILD-BRIEF.md` in full. Items 4 and 5 are your instructions. The rest is the contract you check the build against, and where it says 'you' outside those items it means the builder. The harness addendum applies to you as well." At spawn time the builder appends one line: the module, the round, and the commit hash being graded. Nothing else in the file changes between rounds.

   **Decisions record.** These are the known hard problems for this kind of software; each is where a build goes wrong when the first thing that compiles gets picked. The word in code type is the record's file name:
   - `renderer`: rendering on a phone-class graphics chip with 8 GB of memory shared between the game and its graphics: which of the engine's renderers, draw-call and triangle budgets, instancing, level of detail, culling.
   - `lighting`: physically based materials, sun and sky, shadows, atmospheric depth, baked versus real-time lighting.
   - `quality-profiles`: how one level carries several quality tiers and how source art is reduced for each, so that a better-looking tier later is a new profile file with no change to levels or code.
   - `landforms`: how the worlds are generated at build time and then fixed: which Geological Survey elevation product and resolution the terrain starts from and how it is compressed; how cliffs, arches and overhangs, which a height map can't hold, are added (sculpted, generated, assembled from scanned pieces); the rules that scatter rock and plants; which lidar and city building records the two cities start from, and how city blocks are put together from building parts; how the result is stored so it can be made again; and how collision stays true to what the player sees.
   - `tick-and-frame`: the simulation step against the drawn frame, interpolation, and what has to be deterministic.
   - `replay`: how an input recording reproduces on the same machine, what survives a move to a different machine, and what a ghost stores so that it plays back anywhere.
   - `player-body`: kinematic versus physics-driven, slopes, steps, moving surfaces, keeping momentum through chained moves, and independence from the active camera.
   - `movement-numbers`: how far, how high and how fast the body goes, between what a trained athlete can do and what feels good to play.
   - `traversal-detection`: finding ledges, vaultable obstacles and runnable walls in arbitrary geometry or from authored markers; corners, inclines and false positives.
   - `animation-contact`: hands on ledges, feet on steps and landings across obstacles of varying size, and a first-person view that stays steady on the same body.
   - `cameras`: third-person collision and occlusion, first-person comfort, switching views in the middle of a move.
   - `character-sourcing`: a realistic human and motion-captured traversal moves under the asset policy, fitting animation made for one skeleton to another, and what a player can change about the character's look (body, face, skin, hair, clothing, colours) with assets the policy allows, so that every choice works with every move in both views.
   - `course-runs`: producing recorded runs that complete a course without a human player and without input no human could give, and laying out routes that meet the flow number.
   - `asset-pipeline`: texture memory, build size, import settings, licence records, and how large files travel so that a fresh clone from GitHub is complete. GitHub refuses any file over 100 MB and Git LFS is not installed on the Fedora laptop, so one option is to keep large source art out of git and fetch it again from the addresses in `ASSETS.md`, checked against recorded checksums.
   - `sound`: footsteps by surface, wind that follows speed, spatial placement, mixing.
   - `input-latency`: what adds delay between the engine receiving input and the picture changing, for keyboard with mouse or trackpad and for a gamepad.
   - `neo-proxy`: predicting the Neo from a development machine with a faster chip, a fan and a different graphics driver (Vulkan on Linux, Metal on macOS), and the margin a proxy number must clear.
   - `mac-delivery`: exporting for macOS from Linux, and what signing or approval the receiving Mac needs without a paid developer account, both for a build copied over by the remote loop and for one handed to someone as a file.
   - `remote-test`: running a build on a machine the builder isn't sitting at, over Tailscale and SSH: getting it there, starting it on that machine's own screen from a remote shell, bringing the results back, what makes a remote number honest (display awake and unlocked, the game's window in front, power source, low-power mode, heat), and never taking the screen from someone who is using the machine.
   - `windowing`: being a good citizen under any window manager, tiling or not, on Wayland and on macOS: pointer capture and release, fullscreen, display scaling, window decorations that some window managers draw and others leave to the game, and laying out the picture and the interface at whatever shape the window is given.
   - `multiplayer-readiness`: what a single-player design must and must not do so that several players can later share one world over a local network or Tailscale: which state belongs to a player and which to the world, how many bodies a level can hold, and how moving hazards and collectibles would stay in step.
   - `verification-rendering`: real frames from the real graphics chip in an automated run (the engine's headless mode swaps in a headless display driver, so it can't be assumed to draw), capturing at a preset's resolution when the window manager, not the game, decides the window's size, and engine failures reported to exit 0 all the same.

   For each, before choosing anything, write `docs/decisions/<problem>.md` with these fields, none empty:
   - **Options:** at least two, one line each. Where a proven project under a licence the policy allows already solves the problem, adopting or adapting it is one of them.
   - **Sources:** the documents consulted this run, each with its address (a URL or a file path); the excerpts that informed the choice quoted verbatim into `docs/survey/<problem>.md`. Exemplars worth studying: Godot's documentation and official demo projects (MIT); the "Abandoned Spaceship" graphics demo for Godot 4 (listed as MIT); the "Desert Light" graphics demo for Godot 4 (no licence found, so study only); the Bistro scene ported to Godot 4 (CC BY 4.0, so anything from it ships with credit); Terrain3D (MIT); Jeh3no's state-machine first-person controller for Godot 4 (MIT, GDScript); Gab-ani's Parkour and Advanced Movement for Godot 4 (code MIT; its animations appear not to be free to ship); DeiranZ's parkour first-person controller for Godot 4 (MIT, GDScript); the NOSHOT first-person parkour controller for Godot (MIT, written in C#, so anything taken from it is ported); the GDQuest third-person controller demo for Godot 4 (code MIT; art CC BY-NC-SA, which the asset policy excludes); Traverser, a traversal toolkit for Unity (MIT); Splash Damage's GDC talks on the freedom-of-movement system in Brink. anandamous's ParkourCharacterController is GPL-3.0 and off limits. The licences given were read from each project's GitHub listing or page when this brief was written, some at second hand, and where none is given it is unknown; confirm each before relying on it. Source under a licence the policy allows may be read, adapted and copied, with its licence notice kept and the copy recorded in `ASSETS.md`. Everything else is studied for behaviour and design through docs, issues and design notes; source under a strong copyleft licence (GPL, AGPL) is off limits to read or copy. If the harness has no web access at all, write "source: training knowledge, unverified" and say so; that line is unavailable once any web search has succeeded in this build, and a source is never invented.
   - **Choice** and **Reason.**
   - **Licence** of everything the choice depends on.
   A record with a field missing is not a decision. A module cannot close until the record for every problem it depends on is complete, and the critic checks the records before anything else.

2. **Build the verification loop before the product.** A verification tool, tracked as the module `verify`, that starts the game on the real graphics chip in test mode, loads a named preset (a level or fixture scene; a camera pose, a camera path or a recorded run; time of day; quality profile; window size), drives it through the engine's input map, and writes PNGs (a still, or for motion a strip of frames), for sound presets a WAV of the game's audio output, and a JSON log with the engine's errors and warnings, the graphics adapter and driver in use, the build's commit, the quality profile and render scale, the frame-time histogram, draw calls, triangle count, process and video memory, the input-to-motion histogram from an input harness that injects input events and measures the time to the body's first resulting movement on screen, which sounds played with their peak level and any clipping, and the diff numbers below. Frame-time numbers from a software renderer don't count toward the budget, and the log is what says which renderer drew. A build step exports the game for macOS, Linux arm64 and Linux x86_64, and for Windows x86_64 as a check that nothing has ruled it out, and records each export's result, its size and a list of every file packed into it.

   The exported game carries a benchmark mode, started from its menu or from the command line, that runs every benchmark path and recorded run and writes the same PNGs and log to a folder a person can copy off the machine. Frame numbers come from camera paths, which don't depend on physics; for each recorded run the log says whether it completed on that machine, so a run that plays out differently there is reported, not hidden. That is how numbers arrive from any machine other than the one the builder is on.

   **The remote loop.** The Neo is on Nick's Tailscale network and answers SSH, so testing on it is one command from the development machine. It needs nothing installed on the Neo beyond Tailscale and macOS's own Remote Login: no Godot, no git, no copy of this repository. A test machine is described by a file, `docs/targets/<name>.json`: its SSH host name, operating system, display resolution, and the one folder this build may use there. `neo` is the first, and `t14` is added the same way when Nick puts that machine on the network. The verification tool's `remote` command takes a target and one of five verbs:
   - `check`: whether the target answers, whether someone is logged in at its screen, whether the display is awake and unlocked, and how long since anyone last touched it.
   - `send`: export the build for that target and copy it over.
   - `bench`: `send`, run the benchmark mode there, and bring the result folder back.
   - `play`: `send`, then open the game on the target's screen for a person to play.
   - `fetch`: bring back any result folder on the target that isn't here yet.

   Result folders land in `docs/<target>/<date>-<commit>/`, each a new folder. The build goes into the same place on the target every time, so old builds don't pile up there, and the command touches nothing else on that machine. The Neo is a boy's own laptop: `bench` and `play` never start while it has been touched in the last five minutes. They say so and leave, unless a person runs them with `--now`, which the builder never passes. A remote number is honest only if the game was really on screen, so the benchmark log records, for the whole run, whether the display was awake and unlocked and the game's window in front, the refresh rate, the power source and whether a low-power mode was on. A run that fails that is kept, labelled void, and counts for nothing. The command is proved before it is trusted: copy, run and fetch against a target that is a folder on the development machine, and `check` against a host that doesn't answer, which must fail in one plain line.

   When a target doesn't answer or isn't free, nothing breaks. The need goes into `docs/WAITING.md` and the manual route stays open: every build in `builds/` carries the benchmark mode, and Nick can run it on that machine and copy the folder into `docs/<target>/` himself.

   Fixture files for the presets are written first, into `docs/fixtures/`: a metrics course of flat ground with gaps, walls and ledges in stepped, known sizes; a benchmark camera path format; and one known-answer fixture per instrument, each written when its instrument is. Presets live one per file in `docs/presets/`; they are added, never edited, and only the critic retires one. Every look preset and every preset that feeds a budget number uses the `neo` profile at the Neo's display resolution, and a still and the frame-time numbers beside it come from the same run, so a lower render scale shows in the picture the critic grades. The presets must include: for each level, a wide view, a view at route distance and a surface close-up, each at two times of day; first-person and third-person frame strips of every move on the metrics course, and along each level's main route both as a gray-block course and dressed; every route's recorded run; the fuzz run on every course; the soak run on every level; a sound preset for each kind of sound; the window sequence across four window shapes (a narrow column a third of the display's width, a half, a short wide strip, and full screen); two bodies in one level, each driven by a different recorded run and, once `character` closes, each wearing a different look; and one level loaded under a second, throwaway quality profile, to show that a better tier is a new profile file and nothing else. Test mode may differ from a normal launch only in seed and timing: the same preset captured both ways, driven by the same input harness, must match within the noise the log reports, and that pair is part of the evidence.

   Every number in item 1 has an instrument, each proved on a fixture with a known answer before its numbers are trusted:
   - Frame time: the histogram, proved against a frame limiter set to a known rate.
   - Memory: readings before load, after load and after unload, proved by loading a texture of known size.
   - Build size and the packed-file list: the build step, proved on a package holding a file of known size and one planted file that `ASSETS.md` doesn't list.
   - Input to motion: the histogram, proved against a fixed synthetic delay.
   - Movement numbers: measured on the metrics course and written to `docs/metrics.json`; the jump proved against a body launched at a known speed under known gravity, ledge reach and wall run by reporting the largest stepped piece that succeeds and the first that fails.
   - Fuzz: proved on a fixture with a deliberate hole in the floor, which it must find.
   - Route completion: proved on a fixture course with one impossible gap, which must fail.
   - Input rate: counted from the recording, proved on a synthetic recording of known rate.
   - Leeway: the recorded run replayed with each take-off shifted earlier and later in the smallest steps the game loop allows, proved on a metrics-course gap whose window can be calculated.
   - Grades: proved on metrics-course pieces of known grade.
   - Restart: time from the fall trigger to control regained, proved against a scripted delay.
   - Flow: the speed trace of the recorded run, proved on a synthetic trace.
   - Soak: first minute against last, proved on a fixture that leaks on purpose.
   - Image numbers: proved on an image against itself and against a copy altered by a known amount.
   - Sound: proved on a tone of known level.
   - Window: the resize, focus-loss and fullscreen sequence driven during a recorded run, with the window manager it ran under named in the log, and the interface check proved on a fixture that has one element placed outside the window.
   - Two players: the two-body preset, proved on a fixture where one body's run is impossible, which must be reported as a failure for that body alone.
   The instruments arrive in two groups, because most of them need a game to measure. `verify` closes on the capture tool, the build step, the benchmark mode, the remote loop, the reference set, and the instruments that need no player: frame time, memory, build size and the packed-file list, image numbers, sound, and the window sequence on an empty scene.
